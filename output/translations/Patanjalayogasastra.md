## 4.22 — citer apratisaṃkramāyāḥ…

**Sūtra.** When the power of consciousness (*citiśakti*), which does not undergo
transition, attains the form of that [intellect], there arises the cognition of one's
own intellect (*svabuddhisaṃvedana*).

**Bhāṣya.** For the power of the enjoyer (*bhoktṛśakti*) is unchanging and does not
undergo transition; yet, once it has — as it were — transitioned into an object that
is itself changing, it follows that object's operation. And because the operation of
the intellect (*buddhivṛtti*) has taken on the form of having grasped consciousness
(*caitanya*), the cognitive operation (*jñānavṛtti*) is said to be non-distinct from
the operation of the intellect — merely by way of imitating it. And so it has been
said:

> Not the netherworld, nor the crevices of the mountains,
> nor the darkness, nor the hollows of the oceans —
> [not there is] the cave wherein eternal *brahman* lies hidden;
> the wise know it to be non-distinct from the operation of the intellect.

---

## 4.23 — draṣṭṛdṛśyoparaktaṃ cittam…

**Sūtra.** The mind (*citta*), coloured by both the Seer (*draṣṭṛ* = *puruṣa*) and the
seen (*dṛśya*), takes every object (*sarvārtha*).

**Bhāṣya.** For the mind-organ (*manas*) is coloured by the object that is to be
thought; and being itself an object, it is bound, through an operation that belongs to
it, to the Self (*puruṣa*), the subject (*viṣayin*). Thus this very mind, coloured by
both seer and seen, manifesting now subject and now object, taking on the nature of
both the conscious and the unconscious — though it is in essence an object, appearing
as if not an object; though unconscious, appearing as if conscious — is, like a crystal
(*sphaṭikamaṇi*), said to be "all-purposed."

It is on account of this conformity of the mind [to consciousness] that some, deluded,
say the mind itself is conscious; while others say all this is mind-only (*cittamātra*),
that there is in truth no world of cows and pots and their causes. They deserve our
compassion. Why? Because the seed of their delusion is itself a mind that shines forth
in the shape of all appearances. — In the insight of concentration (*samādhiprajñā*),
the object to be known stands as a reflection (*pratibimba*), and, because it serves as
the support of that insight, it is something distinct [from the insight]. Were that
object mere mind, how could the form of insight be ascertained by insight alone? The
Self, then, is that by which the reflected object is ascertained within the insight.
Thus, since the mind differentiates the natures of grasper, grasping, and grasped,
those of right vision (*samyagdarśin*) distinguish all three as distinct in kind; and
by them the Self is reached.

## 4.24 — tad asaṃkhyeyavāsanābhiḥ…

**Sūtra.** Though variegated by countless latent traces (*vāsanā*), [the mind] exists
for the sake of another (*parārtha*), because it acts as an aggregate
(*saṃhatyakāritva*).

**Bhāṣya.** This mind, though made variegated by countless latent traces, exists for
another's sake — for the experience (*bhoga*) and release (*apavarga*) of another, not
for its own sake — because it acts as an aggregate, like a house. A mind that acts as
an aggregate cannot exist for its own sake: a pleasurable mind does not exist for the
sake of pleasure, nor does cognition exist for the sake of cognition; both exist for
the sake of another. And the one who, through experience and release, is the possessor
of that purpose is the "other" — and this "other" is no mere generality
(*sāmānyamātra*). For whatever the Annihilationists (*vaināśika*) might adduce, in its
own right, as an "other" that is a mere generality, all of that, since it too acts as
an aggregate, would itself exist for the sake of another. But that "other" which is the
distinct one (*viśeṣa*) does not act as an aggregate: it is the Self (*puruṣa*).

## 4.25 — viśeṣadarśina ātmabhāvabhāvanānivṛttiḥ

**Sūtra.** For one who sees the distinction (*viśeṣadarśin*), the cultivation of
speculation about self-existence (*ātmabhāvabhāvanā*) ceases.

**Bhāṣya.** Just as in the rains the presence of a seed is inferred from the sprouting
of a blade of grass, so when, on hearing the path to liberation, a person's hair
bristles and tears fall, one infers that here too there is present the seed of
discernment of the distinction — accomplished by action conducive to release
(*apavargabhāgīya karman*). In such a person the cultivation of speculation about the
self proceeds naturally. (It is for want of this seed that the verse was uttered: that
those who, abandoning their true nature through the faults [of delusion], take pleasure
in the prior position and feel aversion toward the settled conclusion, indulge that
speculation —) namely: "Who was I? How was I? What is this? How has it come to be? What
shall we become? How shall we be?" But for one who sees the distinction it ceases. Why?
Because this is merely a variegated transformation (*pariṇāma*) of the mind, whereas the
Self, once ignorance (*avidyā*) is absent, is pure and untouched by the mind's
attributes. Hence for the adept this speculation about the self comes to rest.

---

## 4.26 — tadā vivekanimnaṃ kaivalyaprāgbhāraṃ cittam

**Sūtra.** Then the mind, inclined toward discrimination (*viveka*), gravitates toward
isolation (*kaivalya*).

**Bhāṣya.** At that point, the mind that had been gravitating toward objects and
inclined toward ignorance becomes otherwise: now it gravitates toward isolation and
inclines toward the knowledge born of discrimination (*vivekaja-jñāna*).

---

## 4.27 — tacchidreṣu pratyayāntarāṇi saṃskārebhyaḥ

**Sūtra.** In its gaps, other cognitions (*pratyaya*) [arise] from latent impressions
(*saṃskāra*).

**Bhāṣya.** For the mind that is now inclined toward the discrimination of cognitions
and flows on as nothing but the discernment of the difference between *sattva* and
*puruṣa* (*sattvapuruṣānyatākhyāti*), there arise — in the gaps of that flow — other
cognitions: "I am," or "this is mine," or "I know," or "I do not know." Whence? From
prior latent impressions whose seeds are being exhausted.

---

## 4.28 — hānam eṣāṃ kleśavad uktam

**Sūtra.** Their elimination is said to be like that of the afflictions (*kleśa*).

**Bhāṣya.** Just as the afflictions, their seeds once burnt, are no longer able to
sprout, so a prior latent impression whose seed has been burnt by the fire of knowledge
(*jñānāgni*) no longer gives rise to a cognition. The latent impressions of knowledge
itself, however, merely lie dormant until the mind's task (*cittādhikāra*) is finished,
and so they are not a matter of concern.

---

## 4.29 — prasaṃkhyāne 'py akusīdasya…

**Sūtra.** For one who, even in higher reflection (*prasaṃkhyāna*), takes no interest
(*akusīda*), there arises — through discriminative discernment (*vivekakhyāti*) in every
respect — the cloud-of-dharma concentration (*dharmamegha-samādhi*).

**Bhāṣya.** When this brahmin (*brāhmaṇa* = the adept) takes no interest even in higher
reflection, he asks nothing further even of that. Being dispassionate toward that too,
he has only discriminative discernment, in every respect; and since the seeds of the
latent impressions (*saṃskāra-bīja*) are destroyed, no further cognitions arise for him.
Then there comes to him the concentration named "cloud of dharma" (*dharmamegha*).

---

## 4.30 — tataḥ kleśakarmanivṛttiḥ

**Sūtra.** From that, the cessation of the afflictions (*kleśa*) and of karma.

**Bhāṣya.** On its attainment, the afflictions beginning with ignorance are scoured out,
root and all, and the karmic residues (*karmāśaya*), good and bad alike, are struck down
to the root. With the ceasing of afflictions and karma, the wise one is liberated even
while still living (*jīvanmukta*). Why? Because error (*viparyaya*) is the cause of
[continued] existence; for no one whose error is exhausted is ever seen to be born,
anywhere, by anything.

---

## 4.31 — tadā sarvāvaraṇamalāpetasya…

**Sūtra.** Then, because of the infinitude of knowledge — freed of every taint of
obscuration (*āvaraṇamala*) — the knowable (*jñeya*) is but little.

**Bhāṣya.** For one released from all the obscurations of affliction and karma,
knowledge becomes infinite. The infinite knowledge-*sattva* (*jñānasattva*), [normally]
overwhelmed and veiled by the obscuring *tamas*, is here and there set in motion by
*rajas*, uncovered, and made capable of apprehension; but when it is rid of all the
taints of obscuration, then it attains its infinitude. And because knowledge is
infinite, the knowable turns out to be little — like a firefly in the [vast] sky.
Whereon it is said:

> A blind man pierced the gem;
> one with no fingers threaded it;
> one with no neck put it on;
> and one with no tongue sang its praise.

---

## 4.32 — tataḥ kṛtārthānāṃ pariṇāmakramasamāptiḥ…

**Sūtra.** Thereupon the constituents (*guṇa*), having accomplished their purpose
(*kṛtārtha*), bring to a close their sequence of transformation (*pariṇāmakrama*).

**Bhāṣya.** With the rising of that "cloud of dharma," the sequence of transformation of
the constituents — now that their purpose is accomplished — is brought to completion;
for once experience (*bhoga*) and release (*apavarga*) have been provided and the
sequence is finished, they cannot persist for even a moment.

---

## 4.33 — kṣaṇapratiyogī pariṇāmāparāntanirgrāhyaḥ kramaḥ

**Sūtra.** Sequence (*krama*), the correlate of the moment (*kṣaṇa*), is apprehended at
the terminus (*aparānta*) of transformation.

**Bhāṣya.** Sequence, whose nature is the uninterrupted succession of moments, is grasped
through the terminus — the end-point — of a transformation; for the "oldness" of a cloth
does not arise at its end without an [implied] sequence of moments that has been
undergone. And a sequence is observed in eternal things too. Now this eternality is of
two kinds: immutable eternality (*kūṭasthanityatā*) and eternality-in-transformation
(*pariṇāminityatā*). Immutable eternality belongs to the Self (*puruṣa*);
eternality-in-transformation belongs to the constituents. That in which, as it is
transformed, the [underlying] reality (*tattva*) is not annulled is eternal; and because
the reality of both [Self and constituents] is never destroyed, both have eternality.

Now, among the constituents' properties — the intellect (*buddhi*) and the rest — the
sequence apprehended at the terminus of transformation does reach an end; but in the
eternal property-bearers (*dharmin*), the constituents themselves, it reaches no end.
And in the immutably eternal liberated Selves (*muktapuruṣa*), established in their bare
own-form, the very being of that own-form is experienced only through a sequence — yet
there too the sequence reaches no end; it is merely a construction, posited by tacking
on the verb "to be" behind the word [that names them].

Now then: of this transmigration (*saṃsāra*), present in the constituents through their
persistence and their motion, is there or is there not an end to the sequence? — "This
is not to be answered (*avacanīya*)." How so? There is the question to be answered
categorically (*ekāntavacanīya*): "Will everyone born die?" — "Yes indeed!" Then there
is the question to be answered with a distinction (*vibhajyavacanīya*): "Will everyone,
having died, be born again?" — one in whom discernment has dawned, whose craving is
spent, the adept, will not be born again, but the other will. And likewise, when one
asks, "Is human birth better, or not?", the question is to be answered with a
distinction: better with respect to beasts, but not with respect to the gods and the
seers. But *this* question — "Is this transmigration finite or infinite?" — [is treated
as] not to be answered. [Yet in truth:] for the adept (*kuśala*) there is an end to the
sequence of transmigration, but not for the other; and since there is a fault in
restricting it to either one alone, this question is in fact one to be answered
analytically (*vyākaraṇīya*).

---

## 4.34 — puruṣārthaśūnyānāṃ guṇānāṃ pratiprasavaḥ…

**Sūtra.** Isolation (*kaivalya*) is the involution (*pratiprasava*) of the constituents
(*guṇa*) now emptied of any purpose for the Self (*puruṣārtha*); or, it is the power of
consciousness (*citiśakti*) abiding in its own nature (*svarūpapratiṣṭhā*).

**Bhāṣya.** The involution — the return to their source — of the constituents, which
consist of cause and effect, once they have furnished experience (*bhoga*) and release
(*apavarga*) and so are emptied of any further purpose for the Self: that is isolation.
"Abiding in its own nature," on the other hand, [means this]: since it is no longer
bound up with the *sattva* of the intellect (*buddhisattva*), the Self's power of
consciousness alone remains, isolated (*kevalā*); and its remaining forever just so is
isolation.

*Colophon.* — Here ends the fourth chapter, the Chapter on Isolation
(*kaivalyapāda*), in the venerable Pātañjala *Yogaśāstra*, the *Sāṃkhyapravacana*, with
the *bhāṣya* of Vyāsa.
